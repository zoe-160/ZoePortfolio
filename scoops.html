01

The design challenge

Scoops started as my New Media capstone at the University of Tampa. I wanted to create something for children that made reading feel more interactive, while also giving them space to be creative. As I continued working on it, the project became less about designing a few good screens and more about figuring out how reading, learning, and creating could all live inside the same product without feeling disconnected.

The experience needed to let a child find a story, practice a skill, make something of their own, and come back later without having to figure the product out again. At the same time, the adults supporting them would need different information, especially around setup, progress, and learning.

For young readers, I focused on making the main actions obvious, keeping the navigation predictable, and using language that felt easy to understand without making the experience feel boring.

For adults, I wanted setup and progress to be easy to find without letting those needs take over the child-facing experience.

02

From map to product

The project started with mapping the main experience in Figma, but once I began coding it, building became another part of the design process.

The early prototype helped me establish the main areas of Scoops—Home, Learn, Create, Friends, profile, and the lesson paths. Once I started turning those screens into a working front end, I began noticing questions that were much easier to ignore in Figma. I had to think about what an empty library looks like, where progress should live, how an adult enters the product, and what should happen before a child begins a learning path.

01Define the core loop — discover, learn, create, return.
02Map the major destinations and connected flows in Figma.
03Build the interface and expose missing states, edge cases, and hierarchy problems.
04Refine navigation, account roles, learning entry points, and progress visibility.
05Keep iterating on the working product instead of treating the prototype as the finish line.

The earlier screens stay in this case study because they show where the project started. The current version looks very different, and that difference matters because it shows how much the thinking changed once the product had to actually work.

03

Designing for children and adults

One thing I became more aware of as Scoops grew was that a child, parent, and teacher should not all be pushed through the exact same experience. For children, I kept the main navigation centered around Home, Learn, Create, and Friends. Setup, diagnostics, progress, and other forms of oversight live in supporting areas instead of competing with those main actions.

As I continued developing the product, I became more intentional about separating those roles. Parent or guardian and teacher entry points are different, and the diagnostic now gives the reader a clearer path from setting up an account into the learning experience.

Clarity mattered throughout the design. I used consistent navigation, larger targets, straightforward labels, visible progress, and read-aloud controls so a young user would not have to guess what to do next.

Keeping each part of the platform connected was important too, so the same color, typography, card, and interaction patterns repeat across reading, learning, creating, and account experiences.

04

What changed once I built it

Moving from Figma into HTML, CSS, and JavaScript changed the way I looked at the design.

A screen can look finished in Figma while still leaving a lot of questions unanswered. Once I started building Scoops, I had to deal with drafts, empty libraries, progress states, preview behavior, account roles, and the transition from a diagnostic into a learning path. I also had to think more seriously about reusable pieces because every new feature needed to feel like it belonged to the same product instead of becoming its own separate screen.

05

What I learned

One of the biggest things Scoops taught me is that product design does not stop once a Figma prototype looks finished.

Building the product made me think more carefully about how visual design, interactions, content, and code affect each other. I stopped looking at each screen as its own thing and started thinking more about how Scoops could continue growing without becoming confusing or inconsistent.

01Implementation can be a design tool. Coding exposed decisions that static screens did not.
02Consistency becomes more valuable as a product grows. Reusable patterns made new features easier to place and understand.
03Different users need different levels of complexity. The child experience and adult experience should feel related without being identical.

If Scoops continues developing, the teacher experience is one of the main areas I want to build out next. Testing the product with children, parents or guardians, and educators would also help show where the navigation, diagnostic, story creation, and progress features make sense in practice and where they still need work.
